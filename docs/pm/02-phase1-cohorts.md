# Phase 1 — User Archaeology

> **Question this phase answers:** Who is actually struggling enough to pay for this — described not as a persona, but as a situation? What just happened in their week that made them open a search tab?

The four cohorts below are described as **situations, not personas.** No names, no ages, no job titles standing alone — only specific moments of friction, the job in progress, the workaround they're operating today, the stakes if they fail, and an evidence flag indicating whether we actually have evidence this person exists or whether we're guessing.

---

## Cohort A — The Sport Rider Recovering From Setback

**Situation sentence:**
*A competitive amateur or semi-pro dressage / show-jumping rider is trying to bring their horse back into work after a 2–6 week injury layup, when they cannot tell whether the horse's current training intensity is sustainable or pushing toward re-injury, and the consequence of failing is either re-injuring a horse they cannot replace, or being too cautious and losing the season's competition calendar.*

**Triggering situation:** Vet has cleared the horse for "light work, gradually increasing" — but "gradually" is qualitatively defined. The rider has no objective signal of whether week 2 of return-to-work is too much.

**Job in progress (JTBD):** *"Help me decide, every day, whether yesterday's training was too much for this horse — before today's session — so I don't undo six weeks of recovery."*

**Current workaround (operation-level):**
- Visual assessment at grooming — checking for heat in the leg, asymmetric muscle tightness
- Lunging for 5 minutes pre-ride and watching for lameness
- Asking the vet via WhatsApp "is it normal that he's a bit stiff this morning?"
- Cross-referencing against personal Whoop / Apple Watch recovery score "if I'm tired, maybe he is too" — magical thinking
- Maintaining a paper training log with subjective 1–10 effort/recovery scores

**Stake on failure:**
- Re-injury → 3–6 more months of layup → loss of competition season → loss of training fees already paid → emotional + financial cost typically €5,000–€20,000 depending on the level
- Career consequence for semi-pros: missed qualifying events
- Relationship cost: vet may attribute the re-injury to rider pushing too hard

**Evidence basis:** `[ASSUMED]` — derived from sport-rider trade publication content (Eurodressage, Horse & Hound) and Whoop-for-athletes literature; **not** from direct interviews. Verify with file 50 interviews.

### Switching interview reconstruction (Cohort A)

- **First thought:** Vet cleared the horse. Felt good for a day. Then the horse seemed stiff on day 3 and the rider didn't know if that was normal stiffness or a warning.
- **Passive looking:** Followed equine-rehab Instagram accounts, read forum posts. Didn't search for a product because didn't know one existed.
- **Active looking:** Triggered by *a near-miss* — pushed the horse on day 4, horse felt off, took two days to bounce back. That was the moment they thought "I need something objective."
- **Decision:** Will try a product if it (a) costs less than one vet visit, (b) doesn't require them to install anything they don't know how to install, and (c) shows them *something they couldn't see by looking*.
- **Consumption (stay vs. churn):** They stay if the product surfaces a real warning at least once in the first 90 days that they would have missed. They churn if it shows them green every day for 30 days — because then it feels like wallpaper.

## Cohort B — The Breeder / Yard Manager With Many Horses And Few Hours

**Situation sentence:**
*A breeder or boutique-yard manager responsible for 6–20 horses they don't personally ride is trying to detect the early signs of colic, foaling complications, or behavioral change in any one horse, when they cannot physically walk through every stall every two hours, and the consequence of failing is either losing a horse they own outright (financial), or losing a boarder's trust (reputational).*

**Triggering situation:** They've had **one bad incident** — either personally or in their peer network — within the last 18 months. A horse they thought was fine at 6pm was found colicking at 11pm. The night before still haunts them.

**Job in progress (JTBD):** *"Tell me which of my 12 horses I need to check on right now, so I'm spending my limited attention where it matters."*

**Current workaround (operation-level):**
- Three checks per day: 6am, noon, 6pm — sometimes 10pm if a horse is flagged
- Stable cameras (often Wyze/Reolink) pointed at flagged stalls — owner watches highlights in the morning
- WhatsApp group with the night-shift staff (if they can afford night-shift staff)
- Vet contact in speed-dial; relationship is more important than any product
- For pregnant mares: a foal-alarm collar specifically (Foalert, Equifone) — narrow purpose, kept on top of any other monitoring

**Stake on failure:**
- Losing a brood mare: €15,000–€100,000+ direct loss
- Losing a boarder's horse: lawsuit + reputation destruction in a tight community
- Career: yard managers are hired and fired on word-of-mouth in a 50-person regional network

**Evidence basis:** `[ASSUMED but moderately reinforced]` — yard manager / breeder pain is widely documented in industry trade publications; the *specific* willingness to pay for monitoring (vs. accept the loss as a cost of business) is unvalidated.

### Switching interview reconstruction (Cohort B)

- **First thought:** Years ago — has always known nighttime is the riskiest window.
- **Passive looking:** Has a Wyze cam; has the Foalert. Considers the problem "solved enough."
- **Active looking:** Triggered by an actual loss (theirs or a peer's) within the last 18 months.
- **Decision:** Will pay €100–300/month per yard if the product (a) does not require them to retrain staff, (b) integrates with vet contact, (c) does not produce alert noise that fatigues them.
- **Consumption (stay vs. churn):** Stays if alert noise is low and the product catches one real issue per year. **Churns immediately** if they get 3 false alarms in a week — yard managers value "the alarm means something" over "the alarm catches everything."

## Cohort C — The Performance-Curious Recreational Rider

**Situation sentence:**
*A recreational rider who follows human athletic-performance content (Whoop, Oura, Garmin) is trying to apply the same recovery-and-readiness framing to their horse, when there's no equine-specific product that speaks their vocabulary, and the consequence of failing is they cobble together a partial picture from Equilab + a vague sense of how the horse felt and never feel like they're actually optimizing anything.*

**Triggering situation:** Their human-athletics app showed a "low recovery" score, they made a connection ("am I doing this for my horse?"), searched, found nothing satisfying.

**Job in progress (JTBD):** *"Show me, in language I already understand from my Whoop, whether my horse is in a state to train hard today or whether we should hack out instead."*

**Current workaround (operation-level):**
- Equilab or Equisense during rides — but data is ride-only, not 24/7
- Self-imposed restrictions ("Mondays are rest days, regardless")
- Personal Whoop/Oura as proxy: "I'm tired, he's probably tired"
- Following equine sports-medicine Instagram accounts; reading content that doesn't translate into specific decisions

**Stake on failure:**
- Subclinical overtraining → minor lameness → 2-week vet visit → €300–€800 cost
- Sense of being a worse rider than they want to be
- Emotional load of not knowing — *itself* the stake, separate from any physical outcome

**Evidence basis:** `[ASSUMED — but founder's instinct is strong here]` This is the cohort the demo's design language speaks to. **This is also the highest commercial-risk segment** because their stakes are emotional/optimization, not loss-prevention. Whoop succeeds because human athletes will pay for marginal optimization; whether the same translates to horse owners is unproven.

### Switching interview reconstruction (Cohort C)

- **First thought:** "I track everything about my own training. Why don't I have this for my horse?"
- **Passive looking:** Read articles. Considered Equilab, found it shallow. Considered Equimetre, found it expensive and racing-focused.
- **Active looking:** Triggered by *content marketing*, not a near-miss. They're not in crisis. They're curious.
- **Decision:** Will pay €15–25/month if the product is beautiful, speaks their vocabulary, and produces a *daily insight they can act on*. Will not pay if it requires hardware investment >€200.
- **Consumption (stay vs. churn):** Stays if the product feels like Whoop-for-horses — i.e., delivers a daily readiness score with one specific recommendation. Churns if it feels like a vet tool — too clinical.

## Cohort D — The Worried Owner Of An Aging Or Recently-Ill Horse

**Situation sentence:**
*An owner whose horse is in their late teens or has had a colic / laminitis / Cushing's incident in the last 12 months is trying to detect the next incident before it requires emergency intervention, when they cannot tell which subtle change matters and which is noise, and the consequence of failing is they either bring the vet out for a false alarm (€150 × repetitions) or miss the real one and lose the horse.*

**Triggering situation:** Recent illness or major vet visit. Vet has said something like "watch him carefully for changes over the next few months."

**Job in progress (JTBD):** *"Help me separate the meaningful changes from the normal ups and downs of an old horse, so I know when to call the vet and when to wait."*

**Current workaround (operation-level):**
- Hyper-vigilance — checking the horse 4–6 times a day for a few weeks post-incident
- Calling the vet "just to ask"
- Keeping a notebook
- Worrying out of proportion → fatigue → reduced vigilance → bad timing of next incident

**Stake on failure:**
- Losing a horse the owner is emotionally bonded to after 15+ years
- Crippling vet bills from late-stage intervention
- Guilt — "I should have known"

**Evidence basis:** `[ASSUMED — strong qualitative evidence in forums; commercial WTP unvalidated]` This cohort has the **strongest emotional drive** of all four but the **least clear willingness to pay** because their behavior swings: in the first month post-incident they'd pay anything; 6 months later they may have habituated.

### Switching interview reconstruction (Cohort D)

- **First thought:** During the recent illness.
- **Passive looking:** Asked the vet, asked friends, joined a Cushing's Facebook group.
- **Active looking:** Active right now — but the window is narrow. Within 60–90 days of the incident they're shoppers. After that, they habituate.
- **Decision:** Will pay if the product is positioned as "early warning for the next event" *and* has a credible vet endorsement.
- **Consumption (stay vs. churn):** Stays as long as the perceived risk feels alive. Churns the moment the horse has gone 6 months without incident — unless the product has surfaced *something* meaningful during that quiet window.

## What this phase implies for Phase 2

1. The four cohorts have **materially different jobs.** Sport rider wants recovery data; yard manager wants signal-to-noise; performance-curious wants beautiful insight; worried owner wants reassurance + early warning. A single product cannot serve all four equally — Phase 7 must pick.

2. Cohorts B and D have **loss-prevention** stakes. Cohorts A and C have **optimization** stakes. Loss-prevention pays more. Optimization is more emotionally resonant. The product can lead with one and grow into the other.

3. Cohort C is **the cohort the demo speaks to** (Anna persona). Cohort C is also the **weakest commercial cohort**. This is the central strategic tension this artifact resolves.

4. The pain scenarios in Phase 2 must cover at least 2 scenarios from each of Cohorts A, B, and D, and at most 2 from Cohort C. The founder's instinct will resist this. The data resists it more.
